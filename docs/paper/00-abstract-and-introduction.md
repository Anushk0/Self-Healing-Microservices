## Abstract

Automated root cause analysis in microservice systems is usually evaluated on incidents where the
faulty service and the service reporting the symptom are the same. That framing rewards a system for
identifying *where the alarm is*, which is a weaker capability than identifying *what is wrong*.

We present a self-healing AIOps platform for a four-service payment application in which diagnosis is
**deterministic-first**: a fault-signature match against documented metric thresholds reaches the
conclusion, retrieved historical incidents may raise or lower confidence but can never override it,
and a self-hosted LLM is asked only to narrate a conclusion it has no capacity to change (in the
reported run that narration succeeded on 3 of 21 incidents, timing out on the rest — and the
diagnostic accuracy was unaffected, which is the point). Remediation proposals are
generated by deterministic logic with no LLM involvement, validated against hard safety rules
evaluated without AI, and gated behind a non-bypassable human approval implemented as a blocking
graph interrupt. Approved actions execute against Kubernetes and are then **verified by
re-observation** of the target service — a design intended to make false recovery measurable rather
than assumed, though that capability is not exercised by the replay evaluation reported here.

We construct a 68-incident dataset in which two of four fault types are deliberately cross-service,
split 20/48 into retrieval-memory and held-out evaluation sets with the separation enforced at four
independent points in code, and compare the pipeline against a single-agent LLM baseline that
receives byte-identical inputs and shares every non-diagnostic stage.

On the held-out set, the multi-agent pipeline was correct on 100% of the incidents it answered
(21/21) against 57.1% for the baseline, and on cross-service faults specifically **100% (9/9) against
0% (0/9)**. The two systems were **indistinguishable on same-service faults**, so the entire
difference is attributable to cross-service diagnosis. The mechanism is identified rather than
inferred: on all nine cross-service incidents, retrieval *disagreed with the correct answer*,
returning incidents rooted at the symptom service, and the baseline's own reasoning cites those
retrieved incidents as its justification in 9 of 9 failures.

Two ablations establish the mechanism as a controlled result rather than an interpretation.
Disabling retrieval entirely leaves the pipeline's diagnoses **unchanged** (100% cross-service
accuracy, all 48 verdicts identical) — retrieval contributes no diagnostic accuracy. Permitting
retrieval to override a signature match, i.e. rebuilding the conventional RAG-first arrangement,
collapses cross-service accuracy to **0%** and reproduces the single-agent baseline's numbers
exactly, to four decimal places. **The measured value lies not in the retrieval, nor in the
multi-agent decomposition, but specifically in the refusal to let retrieval decide.**

We report the limits as prominently as the results: detection, not diagnosis, was the binding
constraint (both arms abstained on 56.2% of incidents because the shared Isolation Forest detector
surfaced only 21 of 48); the absolute accuracy figures are bounded by a circularity between the
synthetic corpus and the diagnostic thresholds; and recovery metrics were not measurable in the
replay configuration used. We additionally show that the conventional hallucination metric — citing a
metric absent from the evidence — reports 0% for a baseline that was demonstrably misrepresenting
metrics that *were* present, and propose a stronger measure that reports 9.5% on the same data.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Motivation

When a microservice system fails, the service that raises the alarm is frequently not the service at
fault. A payment service that times out waiting on a notification service is not itself broken. An
authentication service that returns HTTP 200 with `valid: false` in the body produces no error metric
of its own while causing failures elsewhere. Both are ordinary architectural situations, and both
defeat the naive strategy of investigating whichever service is loudest.

This class of incident is also where automated assistance would be most valuable, because it is
precisely where human on-call engineers lose time — following the alarm rather than the cause.

### 1.2 The problem with the obvious approach

The natural design for an LLM-based RCA system is retrieval-augmented: embed past incidents, retrieve
the nearest neighbours for the current telemetry, and let the model reason from that precedent.

This work finds that arrangement to be actively harmful on exactly the incidents that matter.
Retrieval matches on the *surface features of the telemetry it is given* — which, for a cross-service
fault, is the symptom service's telemetry. It therefore returns incidents rooted at the symptom
service, confidently, and a model that reasons from that precedent reproduces the very error the
system was built to avoid.

### 1.3 Contributions

1. **A deterministic-first diagnostic architecture** in which retrieval and the LLM are structurally
   incapable of overriding a signature match, and a measurement of what that constraint is worth: it
   accounts for the entire difference between this system and a single-agent baseline given identical
   evidence (§5.3, §6.1).
2. **A closed loop rather than a diagnostic benchmark.** The system validates, gates, executes, and
   then re-observes the target service to determine whether the fault actually cleared — a design
   intended to make *false recovery* a measurable outcome rather than an assumption (§3.9).
   **This capability was not exercised in the reported evaluation**, which ran in replay mode where
   no fault is active to remediate; recovery metrics are reported as unmeasured (§6.6).
3. **Non-bypassable human approval as an architectural property**, implemented as a blocking graph
   interrupt rather than a policy, with confidence-gated autonomy explicitly rejected and the reasons
   stated (§3.8).
4. **A cross-service fault dataset** with leakage prevention enforced at four independent points in
   code, including a live pre-flight check that aborts the evaluation unless the guard demonstrably
   refuses a real evaluation incident at the moment of measurement (§4.6).
5. **An audited comparison.** Following the withdrawal of the closest prior work for scoring a source
   constant rather than model output, we demonstrate — with a permanent regression test — that our
   scored quantity varies with input telemetry, and disclose that root-cause accuracy is
   arithmetically equal to fault-type accuracy for our system (§5.2 of Related Work).
6. **A negative result about a standard metric.** The conventional hallucination definition reports
   0% for a model that was fabricating the *significance* of real metrics; we propose and apply a
   measure that detects this, reporting 9.5% on the same responses (§6.4).

### 1.4 What this paper does not claim

Stated here rather than buried, because the scope of the claim is narrower than the headline numbers
suggest:

- **Not that multi-agent designs are better at incident response.** On same-service faults the two
  systems were identical, and the multi-agent design cost roughly 3.6× the wall clock. The benefit is
  confined to cross-service diagnosis.
- **Not that the system achieves high accuracy in deployment.** Measured over all incidents rather
  than answered ones, accuracy was 43.8%, because the detector surfaced fewer than half of them.
- **Not that the absolute accuracy figures generalise.** They were obtained on a synthetic corpus
  whose generator shares constants with the diagnostic thresholds; the comparative findings survive
  this, the absolute ones do not (§6.2).
- **Not that the local model is adequate for narration.** It exceeded a 30-second timeout on 18 of 21
  incidents. The system's diagnostic accuracy was unaffected, which is a claim about the architecture
  rather than about the model.
